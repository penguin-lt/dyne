**About this chapter.** Job opens with a prose tale: a non-Israelite worthy in the land of Uz,
his piety, his wealth, and the wager played out on his household. Genre, folktale. Setting,
outside Israel altogether, and the text locates Uz no more precisely than that. Language,
Hebrew. Date, not stated by the text and not guessed here. Text, the Leningrad Codex as served
by Sefaria. Witnesses, versions and lexica were not consulted this session, and every claim in
the notes that would rest on them is marked or left out. Register, the house standard (4.1),
with the anatomy carve-out in force. The received paragraphing is kept: the Leningrad text
breaks the chapter after verse 5 and at the chapter's end. Verse numbers follow the received
Hebrew versification and appear only in the notes.

## The translation

There was a man in the land of Uz, and his name was Job. That man was blameless and upright: he
feared God and turned aside from evil. Seven sons and three daughters were born to him. His
stock was seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels, five hundred yoke of oxen and five
hundred female donkeys, and a very great body of slaves, and that man was greater than all the
sons of the east. His sons went and made a feast, each in his house on his day, and sent and
called their three sisters to eat and drink with them. When the days of the feast had gone
around, Job sent and consecrated them, and rose early in the morning and made burnt offerings
according to the number of them all, for Job said: Perhaps my sons have sinned, and blessed God
in their hearts. So Job did all the days.

Now one day the sons of God came to present themselves before Jahve, and the accuser came
among them. Jahve said to the accuser:

> "Where do you come from?"

The accuser answered Jahve:

> "From roaming the earth, and walking about in it."

Jahve said to the accuser:

> "Have you set your heart on my servant Job? There is none like him on the earth: a man
> blameless and upright, who fears God and turns aside from evil."

The accuser answered Jahve:

> "Does Job fear God for nothing? Have you not fenced him about, and his house, and all that he
> has, on every side? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his stock has burst out in
> the land. But stretch out your hand now, and touch all that he has — surely to your face he
> will bless you."

Jahve said to the accuser:

> "See, all that he has is in your hand. Only against him do not stretch out your hand."

And the accuser went out from the presence of Jahve.

Now one day his sons and his daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their
brother, the firstborn. A messenger came to Job and said:

> "The oxen were plowing, and the female donkeys were grazing beside them. Sabeans fell on them
> and took them, and the slaves too they killed with the edge of the sword. I alone escaped to
> tell you."

While this one was still speaking, that one came and said:

> "Fire of God fell from the heavens and burned among the sheep and the slaves, and consumed
> them. I alone escaped to tell you."

While this one was still speaking, that one came and said:

> "Chaldeans set three heads and swept down on the camels and took them, and the slaves too
> they killed with the edge of the sword. I alone escaped to tell you."

While this one was still speaking, that one came and said:

> "Your sons and your daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their brother,
> the firstborn. And suddenly a great wind came from across the wilderness and struck the four
> corners of the house, and it fell on the slaves, and they died. I alone escaped to tell you."

Job rose and tore his robe and shaved his head and fell to the ground and bowed. And he said:

> "Naked I came out of my mother's womb, and naked I return there. Jahve gave, and Jahve has
> taken. Let the name of Jahve be blessed."

In all this Job did not sin, and did not attribute folly to God.

## The notes

**1:1 "There was a man in the land of Uz"** — The story sets its hero outside Israel's line
altogether, in a land it does not locate more precisely. Job is not an Israelite, and the tale
never treats that as a problem.

**1:3 "a very great body of slaves"** — עֲבֻדָּה is service in the sense of enslaved labor, and
the wealth count includes people. The young men who die at 1:15–19 belong to this body, which
is why the chapter prints them as what they were. Darby's "very many servants" softens the
institution the Hebrew names (4.2).

**1:4 "each in his house on his day"** — The brothers took turns hosting. "His day" may mean a
birthday. The text does not say, and the note does not settle it.

**1:5 "and blessed God in their hearts"** — The Hebrew reads "blessed", and the sense is
"cursed". To bless God was the standing euphemism for cursing him, and the scribes left it
standing in the consonantal text. The same idiom is at 1:11 below and twice more in the book.
The base translation resolves the euphemism to "cursed" at 1:5 and 1:11. The text here prints
what the Hebrew says, and the note carries the idiom. "So Job did all the days" — regularly,
through every cycle of feasts.

**1:6 "the sons of God came to present themselves"** — The divine council, preserved and not
flattened (4.2). "The accuser" — הַשָּׂטָן is a common noun with the definite article at every
occurrence in this chapter: a council office, not a proper name, and it prints lower case by
instruction 33. The contrast with the census seam, where 1 Chronicles 21:1 prints a bare
"adversary" without the article, is reported and not adjudicated (Law VI). That citation is
outside this chapter and stands unverified here.

**1:8 "Have you set your heart on my servant Job?"** — The idiom is "set your heart on", which
most English prints as "considered". The literal is kept. "My servant" is the covenant
honorific for a person in God's service, the one case where 4.2 permits "servant" — and
elsewhere in this same chapter the institution prints as what it is.

**1:9 "Does Job fear God for nothing?"** — חִנָּם. The word is the book's question in one term:
whether there is such a thing as disinterested piety.

**1:10 "Have you not fenced him about"** — The fence image is protection as a wall. The printed
received text carries an editorial bracket around one word here, a Masoretic note in the
printed edition. The rendering follows the received vocalization, and the bracket's full
resolution stands unconfirmed: no critical apparatus was consulted this session. "His stock has
burst out in the land" — the Hebrew verb is the one for water breaking through.

**1:11 "surely to your face he will bless you"** — The euphemism again (1:5). The Hebrew words
are "if not, to your face he will bless you", the oath formula doing the work of "surely". The
English takes the force and not the words, and it does not resolve the euphemism the way the
base translation did.

**1:12 "Only against him do not stretch out your hand"** — The wager's limit: everything Job
owns is handed over, Job's person is not — for now, and the limit moves in chapter 2. That
citation is outside this chapter and stands unverified here.

**1:14 "the female donkeys were grazing beside them"** — עַל־יְדֵיהֶם is literally "at their
hands": beside them. The sex of the animals is in the Hebrew. It is breeding stock being
counted before the raid takes it.

**1:15 "Sabeans fell on them"** — "Fell on" is the raiding idiom, the same verb as the wind at
1:19. Sabeans are a southern people, and the chapter cares only that they came and took.

**1:15 "I alone escaped to tell you"** — רַק־אֲנִי לְבַדִּי, "only I, by myself": the Hebrew
doubles the aloneness. The refrain is word-identical in all four speeches at 1:15, 1:16, 1:17
and 1:19, and the translation keeps it word-identical. Whatever else the messengers' formula
is doing, the repetition is the author's, and it prints.

**1:16 "Fire of God fell from the heavens"** — The messenger names God as the agent outright,
and the translation does not soften the attribution (Law III). Whether the messenger's theology
is the narrator's is not adjudicated here (Law VI).

**1:17 "Chaldeans set three heads"** — שָׂמוּ שְׁלֹשָׁה רָאשִׁים, "set three heads": three raiding
detachments. "Heads" is the Hebrew's own word for the columns, and it prints as the image it
is.

**1:19 "suddenly a great wind came from across the wilderness"** — The shock-marker הִנֵּה (4.1,
Rule 3). The wind comes from the desert side, and the house falls on the drinkers. The
messenger was outside it, which is why he can tell it.

**1:20 "tore his robe and shaved his head"** — The standard mourning acts of the world the
story comes from. Both are physical acts, and both print as physical. "And bowed" — וַיִּשְׁתָּחוּ,
the body-language of worship. The Hebrew names no object, and neither does the translation.

**1:21 "Naked I came out of my mother's womb"** — בֶּטֶן is the womb, the organ (Law II), not an
abstraction. "Naked I return there" — the Hebrew is the imperfect, an open return rather than a
dated one, and the English keeps the present. "Jahve gave, and Jahve has taken" — the grammar
gives the agency to Jahve twice, and no permissive softening is available under Law III. "Let
the name of Jahve be blessed" is the jussive, a summons to praise rather than a report. The
root here is the same one the euphemism hides at 1:5 and 1:11 — used in the open, and meant.

**1:22 "did not attribute folly to God"** — תִּפְלָה is folly or unseemliness. It is not the word
for prayer, which differs from it by one vowel, and the two should not be confused. The Hebrew
says Job did not "give" folly to God. "Attribute" is the English idiom for that giving.

**About chapter 2.** The tale continues in the same prose: the second wager, the strike on the
man himself, the wife, and the arrival of the three friends. The received paragraphing is kept:
the Leningrad text breaks after verse 10, at the close of the exchange with the wife. The
printed text carries editorial brackets on one word at 2:7, as at 1:10, and the note reports
them where they bear on the rendering.

## The translation, chapter 2

Now one day the sons of God came to present themselves before Jahve, and the accuser came
among them too, to present themselves before Jahve. Jahve said to the accuser:

> "Where do you come from?"

The accuser answered Jahve:

> "From roaming the earth, and walking about in it."

Jahve said to the accuser:

> "Have you set your heart on my servant Job? There is none like him on the earth: a man
> blameless and upright, who fears God and turns aside from evil. And he is still holding fast
> his blamelessness — though you incited me against him, to swallow him for nothing."

The accuser answered Jahve:

> "Skin for skin! All that the man has he will give for his life. But stretch out your hand
> now, and touch his bone and his flesh — surely to your face he will bless you."

Jahve said to the accuser:

> "See, he is in your hand. Only spare his life."

And the accuser went out from the presence of Jahve, and struck Job with an evil inflammation,
from the sole of his foot to his crown. He took a potsherd to scrape himself with, and sat in
the midst of the ashes. His wife said to him:

> "Are you still holding fast your blamelessness? Bless God, and die."

He said to her:

> "You speak as one of the foolish women speaks. Shall we take the good from God, and not take
> the evil?"

In all this Job did not sin with his lips.

The three friends of Job heard all this evil come upon him, and came, each from his place:
Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite. They met together to come
lament for him and comfort him. They lifted their eyes from afar, and did not recognize him.
They lifted their voices and wept, each tore his robe, and they threw dust on their heads
toward the heavens. They sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and none
spoke a word to him, for they saw that the pain was very great.

## The notes, chapter 2

**2:1 "Now one day the sons of God came"** — The council scene opens with the same formula as
1:6, and the chapter prints the repeat. The scene's second "to present themselves" — the
Hebrew infinitive does not mark who presents: the whole company, or the accuser alone. English
must choose, and the rendering reads it with the company. The other reading is this note's to
carry.

**2:2 "Where do you come from?"** — The exchange repeats 1:7 almost word for word: the answer
is identical, the question differs by one small deictic particle in the Hebrew (literally,
"where from here do you come"). The answer's word-identity is the author's and prints.

**2:3 "Have you set your heart on my servant Job?"** — The verdict speech repeats 1:8 verbatim
and adds two clauses. "Blamelessness" is from the same root as 1:1 "blameless": God's
description repeats the narrator's first words about Job. "You incited me against him, to
swallow him for nothing" — God says the incitement was aimed at him, and the verb is the one
for instigation, always to something bad. "To swallow him" is destruction as eating. "For
nothing" is the accuser's own word from 1:9, and it recurs here in God's mouth.

**2:4 "Skin for skin"** — The proverb prints as the image it is, and its sense is spelled out
in its second half: all the man has, he will give for his own life. "His life" is נַפְשׁוֹ, the
word 4.2 renders "life / self", never an immortal component.

**2:5 "touch his bone and his flesh"** — The body named as body, bone and flesh (Law II). "He
will bless you" — the euphemism again (1:5, 1:11). The Hebrew says "bless", the sense is
"curse", the idiom prints as written and the note carries it. The base translation resolves
the idiom to "curse". This text does not.

**2:6 "Only spare his life"** — The verb is "keep, guard". The wager's limit moves: at 1:12
Job's person was beyond the accuser's hand, and here only his life is. The movement is
reported, not adjudicated.

**2:7 "an evil inflammation"** — רַע is "evil", the word Law III guards, and it prints where
the base translation softened it to "grievous". The same word stands in the friends' report at
2:11. "From the sole of his foot to his crown" — the body from end to end, printed as a
location and not generalized to "entirely". The printed text carries editorial brackets on
"to" here, as at 1:10. The rendering follows the received vocalization, and the brackets'
resolution stands unconfirmed: no critical apparatus was consulted this session.

**2:8 "a potsherd to scrape himself with"** — The instrument of managing the disease, printed
as the object it is. "In the midst of the ashes" — the grammar allows "the ash heap", the town
burning-place, as well as "the ashes". The literal prints, and the alternative is the note's
to carry.

**2:9 "Bless God, and die"** — The euphemism at its harshest (1:5, 1:11, 2:5): she says
"bless", the sense is "curse", and neither the text nor the note resolves it. "Are you still
holding fast your blamelessness?" — her words take the same verb and the same noun as God's at
2:3. The echo is the author's and prints. The base translation resolves the idiom to
"renounce". This text does not.

**2:10 "one of the foolish women" / "Shall we take the good from God, and not take the
evil?"** — The Hebrew has no interrogative particle in the second clause. The rhetorical force
is carried by the parallelism. "The evil" is רָעָה, Law III's word. "Did not sin with his
lips" — 1:22 said Job did not sin, and this verse narrows the scope to his lips. The
narrator's own statement moves, and the translation prints the movement without refereeing it
(Law VI).

**2:11 "The three friends of Job"** — The book's other principals enter, each named with his
region, and the gentilics stay. "All this evil" — the narration itself calls what came on him
"evil", and prints it. "To lament for him and comfort him" — both verbs print. What the
comfort turns out to be is not this chapter's to say.

**2:12 "did not recognize him"** — From a distance the disease had made the man
unrecognizable, and the verse prints the fact plainly. "Each tore his robe" — the mourning act
Job performed alone at 1:20. "They threw dust on their heads toward the heavens" — the dust is
thrown up and falls back down, and the direction prints as the Hebrew gives it.

**2:13 "seven days and seven nights"** — The mourning period, and the friends' first act is
silence. The verse gives its own reason: "they saw that the pain was very great", and the word
is bodily pain — כְּאֵב — not "grief". It prints as pain.

**About chapter 3.** The prose tale ends here. What opens at 3:1 is the first of the book's
poems: Job's curse of the day of his birth, the shortest and coldest speech he makes. Genre,
curse-song, and the received sections keep the door of it: verses 1-2 are the prose hinge, and
verses 3-26 are the poem, per the open-section marks in the Leningrad text. Layout is stanzas
keeping the parallelism (Law VII). The received section marks divide the prose hinge
(verses 1-2) from the poem (verses 3-26).

---

## The translation, chapter 3

After this, Job opened his mouth and cursed his day. Job answered and said:

> Let the day perish on which I was born,
> and the night that said: A man is conceived.
> That day: let it be darkness.
> Let God above not seek it out, and let no light shine on it.
> Let darkness and death's shadow claim it.
> Let a cloud-mass settle on it.
> Let the blackness of the day terrify it.

> That night: let thick darkness seize it.
> Let it not rejoice among the days of the year.
> Let it not come into the count of the months.
> See, let that night be barren.
> Let no shout of joy come into it.
> Let the day's cursers lay hold of it —
> the ones ready to rouse Leviathan.
> Let its twilight stars go dark.
> Let it wait for light, and there be none.
> And let it not see the eyelids of dawn.

> For it did not shut the doors of my belly,
> and it hid trouble from my eyes.

> Why did I not die from the womb?
> Come out of the belly, and expire?
> Why did the knees receive me,
> and why the breasts, that I should suck?

> For now I would have lain down and been quiet.
> I would have slept. Then there would be rest for me,
> with the kings of the earth and its counselors,
> who built ruins for themselves,
> or with princes who had gold,
> who filled their houses with silver.
> Or I would have been like a hidden stillbirth,
> like infants that never saw the light.

> There the wicked cease from raging.
> There the weary of strength are at rest.
> Together, prisoners are at ease.
> They do not hear the taskmaster's voice.
> Small and great are there,
> and the slave is free of his master.

> Why is light given to the miserable,
> and life to the bitter of self,
> who wait for death, and it does not come,
> who dig for it more than for hidden treasures,
> who rejoice to exultation, and are glad,
> when they find the grave —
> to a man whose way is hidden,
> and whom God has fenced in?

> For my sighing comes before my bread,
> and my roarings pour out like water.
> For I feared a fear, and it has come upon me.
> What I dreaded has come to me.
> I was not at ease. I was not quiet. I had no rest.
> And raging came.

## The notes, chapter 3

**3:1 "opened his mouth and cursed his day"** — The verb is the plain word for cursing, and it
is the first open use of it in the book. Four times the idiom hid "curse" behind "bless" (1:5,
1:11, 2:5, 2:9). What Job was suspected of doing in secret, he now does out loud, with the open
word, and the narrator states it without flinching. The verse ends the prose: everything after
this until chapter 42 is speech.

**3:3 "Let the day perish on which I was born"** — The poem opens in the jussive, the grammar
of command applied to the calendar, and the poem never leaves it: nineteen of its lines wish
something on that day and that night. "A man is conceived" — the word for the man is the strong
one, the male as strength.

**3:4 "Let God above not seek it out"** — "Seek out" is the verb of inquiring after a person.
God is asked to un-notice the day. "God" here is אֱלוֹהַּ, the poetic singular 4.2 notes as
unusual, and this poem uses it more than any other chapter so far.

**3:5 "Let darkness and death's shadow claim it"** — "Claim" is the kin-redeemer's verb, the
one that recovers property for the family. Darkness is asked to redeem the day as its own.
"The blackness of the day" — the noun occurs here alone in the Hebrew Bible, and the note says
so rather than pretending otherwise.

**3:7 "See, let that night be barren"** — The pointer הִנֵּה by function (4.1, Rule 3): the
shock is that a night is being asked to have conceived nothing at all. The counter-image to
3:3, where the night announced a conception.

**3:8 "the ones ready to rouse Leviathan"** — The poem calls for professional cursers, the
kind whose curse works, who know the incantation able to wake the chaos serpent — as if that
day were beyond ordinary cursing. Leviathan is named and not explained. The verse takes the
divine-realm mythology as known to its hearers, and prints it unflattened (4.2). What waking
Leviathan would do is not said here, and the note does not supply it.

**3:9 "the eyelids of dawn"** — Dawn's eyes, with their lashes, are the poem's own body image,
and it prints as given. The verse completes the day's destruction in reverse order: stars,
light, the dawning itself.

**3:10 "it did not shut the doors of my belly"** — בֶּטֶן is the organ (Law II), and the Hebrew
says "my belly", not "my mother's womb" — the mother is supplied by every English tradition,
including the base translation, and is absent here. The day is charged with negligence: it had
one job, closing the womb's doors, and it failed. "Trouble" is the word that comes back at
3:26 and at 3:17.

**3:11 "Come out of the belly, and expire"** — The wish is a birth in reverse: out of the
womb, straight into death. The second verb is the breath going out, and it prints as that.

**3:12 "Why did the knees receive me"** — The newborn was set on the knees to be claimed, and
the verse asks why the claiming happened. "Receive" is the modern of the base translation's
"meet", which has drifted.

**3:14 "who built ruins for themselves"** — The paradox is the Hebrew's own: kings built
ruins, and the English keeps the paradox. Read forward, their monuments. Read backward, their
burial. The note offers the reading and does not choose.

**3:16 "a hidden stillbirth"** — נֶפֶל, the miscarried or stillborn child, printed as the
thing itself (Law II), not folded into euphemism. The infants "that never saw the light" carry
the same image the poem wishes on its own night in reverse.

**3:17 "There the wicked cease from raging"** — The grave as the one place the cycle stops.
"Raging" is the verse's word for the restless trouble of the wicked, and it is the word the
poem hands back at 3:26 with Job as its object.

**3:19 "the slave is free of his master"** — עֶבֶד is "slave" (4.2), and חָפְשִׁי is the
emancipation word. The poem's starkest line: in death the institution lets go. It prints
without commentary.

**3:20 "the bitter of self"** — נֶפֶשׁ is "life / self" (4.2), never an immortal component:
the bitter of self is the person soured through. "Dig for it more than for hidden treasures" —
they dig for death as for buried silver, and the image is the poem's, not the note's.

**3:23 "whom God has fenced in"** — The fence of 1:10, with the parties reversed: there the
accuser complained God had fenced Job about in protection, and here Job names the fence with
God doing the fencing, as siege. Same figure, same root, opposite door. The note reports the
reversal and does not referee it (Law VI).

**3:24 "my roarings pour out like water"** — The word is a lion's roar, not a groan, and the
verb is what water does when the container fails. "Before my bread": the sighing arrives
first, at the table.

**3:25 "I feared a fear"** — The Hebrew uses the same root twice, fear in the abstract and
fear in the act, and the English keeps the doubling. What Job feared is not named. The reader
who supplies "exactly this" is reading the whole book, and this verse does not yet say it.

**3:26 "I was not at ease. I was not quiet. I had no rest. And raging came."** — Three
negations in a row, then the verb from 3:17 — the restlessness that belonged to the wicked
arrives at Job. The poem ends by handing him the wicked's word, and stops there.
